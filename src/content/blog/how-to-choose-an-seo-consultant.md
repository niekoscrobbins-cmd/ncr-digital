Hiring an SEO consultant is a considered-purchase decision most buyers make with very little to compare against. There's no standard credential, no licensing body, and no published price range across the industry, which means the vetting has to happen through the questions you ask and the specifics a vendor is willing to commit to in writing.

## What to Ask Before the First Call Ends

| Question | What a good answer sounds like | What a weak answer sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| "What will you measure, and how often will I see it?" | Specific metrics (indexed pages, ranking position, converting traffic), a stated cadence, and a baseline before work starts | Vague reference to "traffic growth" with no baseline or cadence |
| "Can I see a redacted example of a past technical audit?" | A real crawl output or GSC export, client details removed | "We don't share past client work" with no substitute offered |
| "What happens if the site has an indexation problem you didn't expect?" | A description of how findings get re-prioritized mid-engagement | A fixed, unchangeable deliverable list regardless of what the audit finds |
| "Who specifically does the technical work?" | A named person, ideally on the call | "Our team" with no individual accountable |

## Red Flags Worth Walking Away From

**A guaranteed ranking position or a fixed timeline to page one.** No one controls Google's ranking algorithm, and any vendor promising a specific outcome is either inexperienced or not being straight with you about how search works.

**No mention of measurement until after the contract is signed.** If a vendor can't describe what they'll track and how often you'll see it during the sales conversation, that discipline usually doesn't materialize once the engagement starts either.

**Content-first proposals with no technical audit.** If a vendor wants to start publishing blog posts before running a crawl and checking indexation, they're skipping the step that determines whether that content will ever be found in the first place. See [what a real technical audit actually covers](/insights/what-is-crawl-budget-saas/) for what should come first.

**Reluctance to provide a reference who churned.** Every agency has clients who didn't renew. A vendor willing to explain why, rather than only offering current happy clients, is giving you a more honest picture of what they're actually like to work with under strain.

## What a Real Proposal Should Contain

A proposal built for your site, not a generic template, should reference something specific about your actual technical state: an indexation gap, a Core Web Vitals score, a crawl budget concern. If a proposal reads identically to what any other prospect would receive, it wasn't built from an audit of your site.

It should also state, explicitly, what gets measured and how often you'll see it. [NCR Digital's SEO work](/seo) is built around exactly that principle: the numbers that define success are agreed before any work begins, not discovered after the invoice.
